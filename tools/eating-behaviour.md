# How snakes catch and eat their prey: notes behind "See it eat"

These notes are what the "See it eat" animation in `species/3d/extras.js` (`eat()`, `feedStyle()`) is built on.
They were collected in November 2026 for Eduardo's request: "he eats while wrapped his whole body on the prey, not
a bit, and check if some snakes do or don't... check how it wraps... be precise instead of guessing."

How the sources were checked: the web searches worked, but this session could not open some sites directly
(deepblue.lib.umich.edu and pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov were blocked). Where a paper could not be opened, the notes rely
on its abstract or on the article that reports it, and they say so. Statements marked *(not confirmed)* are common
herpetological knowledge that the sources found here did not state directly.

## 1. Constriction: what really happens

| Step | What the snake does | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Strike | A fast strike. Even harmless rat snakes strike "faster than the blink of an eye" (a blink is about 200 ms). The snake seizes the prey with its jaws. | Penning et al. 2016, reported in [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/scientists-surprise-even-nonvenomous-snakes-can-strike-ridiculous-speeds-180958452/) |
| Coils thrown | Right as the jaws land, the snake turns its head and **wraps loops of its body round the prey**. High-speed video (500 frames/s) of a boa shows the first full loop forming at once, belly against the prey, and the next loop pressing with its side (a "ventral-lateral" coil). Coiling follows the strike within a fraction of a second; it is one continuous, very fast movement, not a slow wrap. | [Boa strike and coil application, 500 fps (Vimeo)](https://vimeo.com/34811637); Moon 2000, J. Zool. 252:83-98 ([Cambridge abstract](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-zoology/article/mechanics-and-muscular-control-of-constriction-in-gopher-snakes-pituophis-melanoleucus-and-a-king-snake-lampropeltis-getula/D30297092962AC1FE1C919BA9877335C)): the back muscles are "highly active during striking and coil formation" |
| How many coils | Usually one to three full loops; small prey can be buried in several stacked loops. Kingsnakes use **regular, fully wrapped coils** laid side by side, and that posture is why they squeeze about twice as hard as rat snakes (about 20 kPa against 10 kPa). Rat snakes' coils are less regular. Greene & Burghardt found 19 different coil patterns in 27 colubrid species, usually one or two per genus. Moon 2000 names three postures: full loops that encircle the prey, partial loops, and loops that only pin it. | Penning & Moon 2017, J. Exp. Biol. ([Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/king-snakes-strength-its-squeeze), [Nature summary](https://www.nature.com/articles/543467c)); Greene & Burghardt 1978, Science 200:74-77 ([record](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/1007219060)); [Wikipedia: Constriction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constriction) |
| Squeeze | Peak pressure is reached **about 6 s after the strike**. It stops the prey's blood flow (circulatory arrest) within seconds, faster than suffocation. Each time the prey breathes out, the snake takes up the slack and squeezes a little tighter. | Boback et al. 2015, J. Exp. Biol. 218:2279 ([PDF](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article-pdf/218/14/2279/1912498/jeb121384.pdf), [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/859705)); the breathing ratchet is from [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/snakes-know-when-to-stop-squeezing-because-they-sense-the-heartbeats-of-their-prey) |
| How long | Boas feel the prey's heartbeat and keep squeezing (harder) until it stops. With a simulated heartbeat they constricted for 22 ± 2 min; kingsnakes in Okstate trials took about 20 s to 4 min. Muscle activity is intermittent during the long squeeze. | Boback et al. 2012, Biol. Lett. 8:473 ([PDF](http://www.stevenpoe.net/uploads/3/7/3/4/37343605/rsbl20111105.pdf)); [The constricting habits of Lampropeltis](https://ojs.library.okstate.edu/osu/index.php/OAS/article/view/4647/4318); Moon 2000 |
| Finding the head, swallowing | When the prey is still, the snake finds the head with tongue flicks and starts swallowing **head first** (limbs and fur fold backwards). Swallowing often starts **while the coils are still round the prey**: they hold it in place and loosen bit by bit as the jaws "walk" forward over it. Loops round the prey during swallowing were seen in most trials with sand boas and rosy/rubber boas (*Eryx*, *Lichanura*, *Charina*). | Mehta dissertation, Univ. Tennessee ([trace.tennessee.edu](https://trace.tennessee.edu/utk_graddiss/1383)); head-first: [Thamnophis fish-feeding thesis](https://trace.tennessee.edu/utk_gradthes/13486) (larger prey taken head first more often). Swallowing while coiled in *Boa*/*Python* themselves: *(not confirmed)* by a source found here, though it is widely filmed |
| Learnt with size | Hatchling corn snakes simply swallow small prey. With bigger prey they add hairpin loops, then full constriction; above a certain prey size every corn snake constricted. | [Prey-handling behaviours of naïve *Pantherophis guttatus*, J. Herpetol. 50:2](https://bioone.org/journals/Journal-of-Herpetology/volume-50/issue-2/14-109/Prey-Handling-Behaviors-of-Na%c3%afve-Pantherophis-guttatus/10.1670/14-109.full); [Smithsonian zoo: corn snake](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/corn-snake) ("then quickly coil themselves around their meal") |

## 2. Snakes that don't constrict

| Group | Behaviour | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Rattlesnakes, other pit vipers, vipers | **Strike and release** a mammal at once (the venom works while it runs), then **follow its scent trail**, find it dead and swallow it head first. No coils. Large individuals hold prey more often. Birds and frogs are often held instead of released. | Kardong ([strike behaviour](https://public.wsu.edu/~kkardong/Web%20of%20KVK_06b/Publications/Predatory_strike_behavior86.pdf), [trailing](https://public.archive.wsu.edu/kkardong/public_html/Web%20of%20KVK_06b/Trailing.htm)); Hayes on *Crotalus oreganus* ([PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/William-Hayes-9/publication/259486904_Stimuli_Influencing_the_Release_and_Aim_of_Predatory_Strikes_of_the_Northern_Pacific_Rattlesnake_Crotalus_viridis_oreganus/links/0046352c31872548e1000000/Stimuli-Influencing-the-Release-and-Aim-of-Predatory-Strikes-of-the-Northern-Pacific-Rattlesnake-Crotalus-viridis-oreganus.pdf)); [rattlesnake strike kinematics, J. Exp. Biol. 201:837](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article/201/6/837/7851/Rattlesnake-strike-behavior-kinematics) |
| Cobras and most other elapids | **Bite and hold** (often chewing to work the venom in) until the prey stops moving, then swallow. Cobras (*Naja mossambica*) struck several times and held rodents in their jaws, letting go only if the prey bit back. No coils. | Kardong strike-behaviour paper above |
| Garter snakes, water snakes (*Thamnophis*, *Nerodia*, *Natrix*) | **Seize and swallow the prey alive**, no constriction (frogs, fish, worms). Big fish are turned and swallowed head first. *T. elegans* is the one garter snake that sometimes constricts, and does it badly. | [Western terrestrial garter snake (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Thamnophis_elegans); [Thamnophis fish-feeding thesis](https://trace.tennessee.edu/utk_gradthes/13486); Ditmars 1912, "non-constrictors swallow the living prey" ([archive.org](https://dn720701.ca.archive.org/0/items/biostor-197303/biostor-197303.pdf)) |
| Racers and whipsnakes (*Coluber*, *Masticophis*, *Hierophis*, *Drymarchon*...) | Seize and swallow alive. They may **press the prey down under a loop of the body** to hold it, which is not true constriction (Moon's "non-encircling loops that pinion the prey"). | Moon 2000 (postures); racer pinning itself: *(not confirmed)* by a source found here |
| Egg-eaters (*Dasypeltis*, *Elachistodon*) | Swallow the egg whole. Spines on the neck vertebrae help crack it inside the throat; they swallow the contents and spit out the crushed shell. | [Britannica: egg-eating snake](https://www.britannica.com/animal/egg-eating-snake); Gans & Oshima 1952 ([archive.org](https://archive.org/download/biostor-194203/biostor-194203.pdf)) |
| Slug and snail eaters (*Dipsas*, *Sibon*, *Pareas*, *Storeria*...) | Seize slow prey without a strike. Gland secretions deal with the slime; some pull snails out of their shells. | [BMC Evol. Biol. 2014 (PMC4021269)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4021269) |

## 3. Behaviour by genus, as used in SnakeList

`feedStyle()` in `species/3d/extras.js` uses this table. A genus that is not listed keeps the old rule: pythons and
boas constrict, other colubrids constrict mammals and birds, and venomous snakes use venom.

| Style | Genera | In the animation |
|---|---|---|
| **Constrictor**: full coils, squeeze, swallow while coiled | All boas and pythons (`plan: python`: *Boa*, *Python*, *Morelia*, *Corallus*, *Epicrates*, *Eunectes*, *Eryx*, *Charina*, *Lichanura*...); *Lampropeltis*, *Pantherophis*, *Elaphe*, *Pituophis*, *Zamenis*, *Coronella*, *Bogertophis*, *Arizona*, *Rhinocheilus*, *Cemophora*, *Euprepiophis*, *Orthriophis*, *Gonyosoma*, *Oreocryptophis*, *Senticolis*, *Spilotes*, *Pseudelaphe*, *Pseustes*, *Phrynonax*, *Boaedon*, *Lamprophis*, *Clelia*, *Oxyrhopus*, *Lycodon* | Strike, then several full coils thrown round the prey in about 0.7 s (stacked in layers when the prey is small), using a large part of the body. The coils tighten in steps with each breath of the prey (about every 0.8 s). The snake keeps the coils on while its tongue finds the head, starts swallowing head first while still coiled, and the coils loosen bit by bit as the jaws walk over the prey. |
| **Seize and swallow alive** | *Thamnophis*, *Nerodia*, *Natrix*, *Regina*, *Storeria*, *Opheodrys*, *Heterodon*, *Xenodon*, *Farancia*, *Liodytes*, *Amphiesma*, *Hebius*, *Rhabdophis*, *Fowlea* | Strike (or seize slow prey), hold, swallow. No coils. |
| **Racers and whipsnakes**: seize, sometimes pin | *Coluber*, *Masticophis*, *Hierophis*, *Dolichophis*, *Ptyas*, *Drymarchon*, *Drymobius*, *Salvadora*, *Psammophis*, *Malpolon*, *Dendrelaphis*, *Ahaetulla*, *Chrysopelea*, *Oxybelis*, *Philodryas* | As above, no constriction. |
| **Vipers and pit vipers**: strike, release, trail | All `plan: viper` and `plan: pitviper` (*Crotalus*, *Sistrurus*, *Agkistrodon*, *Bothrops*, *Bitis*, *Vipera*, *Daboia*...) | A mammal or bird is let go, followed and swallowed head first. Frogs, lizards and fish are held. No coils. |
| **Elapids**: bite and hold | All `plan: elapid` and `plan: sea` (*Naja*, *Dendroaspis*, *Bungarus*, *Micrurus*, *Oxyuranus*, *Pseudechis*, sea snakes...) | Bite and hold until the venom works, then swallow. No coils. |
| **Egg-eaters** | *Dasypeltis*, *Elachistodon* | The egg is seized and swallowed, then cracked in the throat. |
| **Slug, worm and centipede eaters** | see `dietOf()` | Seized without a strike and swallowed. |

## 4. Second check, step by step (v6.20)

Eduardo asked (November 2026): "you need to go see how a snake eats". Each step of the animation was checked again against
the sources above and some new ones. Here is what changed.

| Step | Real behaviour | Before v6.20 | Now |
|---|---|---|---|
| Detection | Tongue flicks follow the scent, and they speed up as the snake closes in. | Present. | Unchanged. |
| Stalk / approach | A slow creep, or a swim. | Present. | Unchanged. The leaves and grass along its path now give way a little. |
| Strike | Fast, about 0.16 s. | Present. | Unchanged. |
| Constriction or venom | Depends on the genus (sections 1-3). | Present. | Unchanged. |
| Finding the head | Constrictors and vipers that track their prey find the head with the tongue. Snakes that **hold** their prey (cobras, garter and water snakes, racers) also shift their grip along the prey to the head before swallowing. | Only constrictors and trackers had this step. | New "grip" step for snakes that hold their prey: the snake eases its bite, works its jaws along with small side-to-side turns and flicks its tongue. |
| Jaw walking | The left and right jaws advance in turn (the "pterygoid walk"), with the head and neck bending to the side as each side moves forward. | Present: alternating jaw `walk` and a small head yaw. | Unchanged. |
| Neck bending / pushing the meal down | Once the jaws are past the prey, the neck bends from side to side and bunches up, concertina-like, to push the meal along. X-ray video of kingsnakes found the pterygoid walk plus "concertina-like compression and extension cycles of the predator's vertebral column" in 2 of 3 trials. Moon 2000 describes lateral bending of the head and neck in gopher and king snakes. | Missing: the body only slid back to rest while the lump moved. | New: lateral waves run down the neck and follow the bulge as it travels down the body. |
| Bulge | A visible lump moves down to the stomach, with the skin stretched between the scales. | Present. | Unchanged; the neck waves now make the push visible. |
| "Yawn" | After a big meal the snake gapes widely, often more than once, and moves the lower jaws from side to side. This resettles the quadrate and the stretched ligaments, and may clear the windpipe. | Missing. | New 2.4 s step: two wide gapes with the lower-jaw halves shifting side to side, then a tongue flick. It is skipped for worms, slugs, centipedes and caterpillars, which are too small for a yawn. |

**Visibility (Eduardo: "you can't even see the snake's prey because its under leafs").** In the leaf-litter habitats
(forest, rainforest, soil), the 3D fallen leaves are now pushed aside in a patch round the prey and the strike and coil
area. They pile up a little at the edge, and a narrower strip clears along the snake's path. In grass and heath the tufts
in that patch are bent down flat and lean outwards. During the show, grass standing between the camera and the action
bows down smoothly, using a view corridor in the plants' vertex shader (`uSlClr` in `nature.js`). The camera
is also closer and higher than before. A 2-3 cm mouse used to be a speck in a wide view of the forest floor. Now
the notice, stalk and strike-pose shots frame the head and the prey together, and the yawn is filmed from the side and a
little in front. Everything springs back when the show ends. Before this, the litter was left untouched, because it counted as "soft" cover. Grass was only
pressed to 16% of its height, which still left 30 cm grass at about 5 cm, taller than a mouse.

New sources:
- Jackson, Kley & Brainerd 2004, "How snakes eat snakes: the biomechanical challenges of ophiophagy for the California kingsnake", Zoology 107:191 ([PDF](https://www.brown.edu/Departments/EEB/brainerd_lab/pdf/Jackson-2004-Zool.pdf), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.zool.2004.06.001)). Covers the four stages (capture, constriction, ingestion, digestion), the pterygoid walk as the main way prey is moved, and concertina-like cycles of the backbone.
- Moon 2000, "The mechanics of swallowing and the muscular control of diverse behaviours in gopher snakes", J. Exp. Biol. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10934002/)). Swallowing begins with lateral bending of the head and neck as the jaws advance one side at a time.
- Kley 2001, prey transport mechanisms in snakes ([PDF](https://medicine.stonybrookmedicine.edu/sites/default/files/anatomy/kleylab/Kley(2001).pdf)).
- The post-meal yawn as jaw realignment: [Australian Geographic, "Why do snakes yawn?"](https://australiangeographic.com.au/?p=219421) and [A-Z Animals on anacondas stretching their jaws after eating](https://a-z-animals.com/articles/why-anacondas-stretch-their-jaws-after-eating-massive-prey/). These are popular sources; no peer-reviewed paper on the yawn was found here *(not confirmed)*.
