Corn Snake

Pantherophis guttatus (Linnaeus, 1766)

The Corn Snake, also called the Red Rat Snake, is a colourful, non-venomous constrictor of the south-eastern and south-central United States. It is one of the most popular pet snakes in the world, and many colour forms have been bred in captivity.[1,2]

Identification

How to recognise it

  • Orange to reddish-brown, with black-edged blotches down the back (about 27 to 41 of them).[5,6]
  • A spear-point mark on top of the head, its tip between or in front of the eyes.[5,6]
  • A black-and-white checkerboard belly, and two black stripes under the tail.[6,7]
  • Regional colours: Snakes from the coastal plain tend to be brighter orange; those from the uplands are darker.[5]

Size

Adults: Adults are usually about 61 to 182 cm (2 to 6 ft) long.[3]

Hatchlings: Babies are about 25 to 30 cm long when they hatch.[8]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: The south-eastern and south-central United States, from about New Jersey south to Florida and west to Texas.[1,3]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Forest edges and woodland, fields, swamps and hardwood hammocks; it also turns up in barns and abandoned buildings, where it hunts rodents.[3,9]

Diet

A constrictor that squeezes its prey. Adults eat mostly rodents and other small mammals, plus birds and their eggs; young snakes also take lizards, small snakes and frogs.[1]

Reproduction

Females lay about 10 to 30 eggs in early summer, which hatch from July to September. The parents give no care to the eggs or young.[4,10]

Who described it

First described by Linnaeus in 1766. The brackets around the authority, (Linnaeus, 1766), mean it was first described in a different genus.

Where the name comes from

Pantherophis: ‘panther snake’, from the Greek roots panther- ‘panther’ + -ophis ‘snake’

guttatus: ‘spotted (as if with drops)’

Worked out from the Latin and Greek roots and the words in the name; parts SnakeList can’t explain are left out.
