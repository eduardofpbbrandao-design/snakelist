Western Hognose Snake

Heterodon nasicus Baird & Girard, 1852

The Western Hognose Snake is a small, stout snake of the North American plains with an upturned, pig-like snout that it uses for digging. It is famous for its acting: when threatened it puffs up and hisses, and may even play dead.[1]

Identification

How to recognise it

  • Upturned, shovel-like snout.[1]
  • Short, thick body with blotches.[1]

Appearance

A short, thick body and an upturned nose scale. Its blotched pattern makes it look like a small rattlesnake, which may help keep enemies away.[1]

Size

Adults: Males are much smaller than females; adults rarely grow longer than about 40 to 50 cm.[1]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: Southern Canada (Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba) south through the Great Plains of the United States to northern Mexico.[1,2]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Sandy or gravelly ground: prairies, river floodplains, scrub, grassland and semi-desert, up to about 2,500 m.[1]

Behaviour

Active mostly by day. When threatened it flattens its neck like a cobra, hisses and makes 'bluff' strikes with its mouth closed; it almost never bites in defence. Hognose snakes may also roll over and play dead.[1]

Diet

Mostly frogs and toads, plus small lizards and the odd rodent. It is not a constrictor: it holds prey and works its rear teeth in.[1]

Reproduction

Lays 4 to 23 long, thin-shelled eggs in June to August; they hatch after about 60 days.[1]

Did you know?

  • When a hognose snake plays dead, it rolls on its back with its tongue hanging out![1]

Who described it

First described by Baird & Girard in 1852.

Where the name comes from

Heterodon: Greek for "different tooth" - after the enlarged teeth at the back of the upper jaw, used to puncture toads that puff themselves up[3]

Western Hognose Snake: ‘Western’ – where it lives (in the west); ‘Hognose Snake’ – its upturned snout looks like a pig's.
