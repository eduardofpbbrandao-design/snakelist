Green Anaconda

Eunectes murinus (Linnaeus, 1758)

The Green Anaconda is the heaviest snake in the world: a huge, non-venomous boa of the rivers and swamps of tropical South America. In 2024 scientists split it into two species, the Southern Green Anaconda (Eunectes murinus) and the Northern Green Anaconda (E. akayima), which look identical but differ in their DNA.[1,2]

Identification

How to recognise it

  • Dark olive-green above, shading to yellow underneath.[5]
  • Dark oval spots along the back; the spots along the sides have yellow centres.[6]
  • A dark stripe runs from each eye back to the corner of the jaw.[5,6]
  • The eyes and nostrils sit on top of the head, so it can watch and breathe while almost completely under water.[3]

Size

Sizes reported: National Geographic gives 6 to 9 m and up to about 227 kg, but the heaviest weights are estimates rather than careful measurements, and a researcher who measured over a thousand wild anacondas found very large ones to be rare.[3,4,7]

Distribution

Published distribution

Before the 2024 split: Tropical South America east of the Andes, in the Amazon and Orinoco basins.[2]

After the split: The Southern Green Anaconda (E. murinus) lives in Peru, Bolivia, Brazil and French Guiana; the Northern Green Anaconda (E. akayima) in Ecuador, Colombia, Venezuela, Trinidad, Guyana, Suriname and French Guiana. SnakeList's map still shows the green anaconda in the older, broad sense.[2,8]

Behaviour

Usually solitary: adults keep to their own hunting areas except in the breeding season.[9]

Diet

A constrictor that can tackle very large animals, including caimans and even jaguars.[4]

Did you know?

  • Its genus name Eunectes means good swimmer.[10]

Who described it

First described by Linnaeus in 1758. The brackets around the authority, (Linnaeus, 1758), mean it was first described in a different genus.

Where the name comes from

Eunectes: Greek for "good swimmer" - fitting for the anacondas[11]

murinus: ‘mouse-coloured’

Green Anaconda: ‘Green’ – its colour.

Worked out from the Latin and Greek roots and the words in the name; parts SnakeList can’t explain are left out.

Classification notes

A 2024 study found that northern and southern green anacondas differ genetically by about 5.5% and split roughly 10 million years ago, and named the northern one Eunectes akayima. Because the two cannot be told apart by appearance, some experts dispute the split.[2,12]
