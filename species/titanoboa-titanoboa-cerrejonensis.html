Titanoboa

Titanoboa cerrejonensis Head et al., 2009

Titanoboa is an extinct relative of today's boas from the Paleocene of Colombia, known only from fossil vertebrae and ribs. Estimated at about 13 m long and over a tonne, it is the largest snake known.[1]

Fossils and geological context

What has been found

Vertebrae and ribs from the Cerrejón coal mine in north-eastern Colombia, belonging to 28 individuals. It was named in 2009.[1]

Geological age and setting

The fossils come from the Cerrejón Formation, dated to about 58 to 60 million years ago (middle to late Paleocene) - some 6 to 8 million years after the non-bird dinosaurs died out.[1]

Size estimate

From the size of its vertebrae, compared with living boas, its describers estimated a body length of about 13 m and a mass of about 1,135 kg. These are estimates, not measurements of a complete skeleton.[1]

What is uncertain

Its describers argued that a snake this large needed a mean annual temperature of at least 30 to 34 °C, so the Paleocene tropics were hotter than today. Other scientists questioned whether today's snakes' temperature limits can be scaled up to Titanoboa, and whether a small sample of backbones shows the species' usual size.[1,2]
