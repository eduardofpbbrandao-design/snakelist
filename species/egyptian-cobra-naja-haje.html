Egyptian Cobra

Naja haje (Linnaeus, 1758)

The Egyptian Cobra is one of the most venomous snakes of northern Africa. Its venom can kill a person. Unlike many African cobras, it does not spit its venom.[1]

Identification

How to recognise it

  • A broad hood spread when threatened.[1]
  • Large, flat head and a thick body.[1]

Appearance

A large, stout cobra with a big, broad head and a hood it can spread. Its colour varies a lot, but most are some shade of brown.[1]

Size

Adults: About 1.4 m long on average; the longest one recorded was 2.59 m.[1]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: North Africa and the savannas of West Africa south of the Sahara, south to the Congo Basin and east to Kenya and Tanzania. Older records from southern Africa and Arabia belong to other cobra species.[1,2]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Steppes, dry and moist savannas and semi-deserts with some water and plants, often near water. It also visits farms and villages, where rats and chickens attract it.[1]

Reproduction

Lays eggs.[2]

Who described it

First described by Linnaeus in 1758. The brackets around the authority, (Linnaeus, 1758), mean it was first described in a different genus.

Where the name comes from

Naja: from the Sanskrit nāga, meaning snake[3]

haje: ‘haje, the Arabic name of the Egyptian cobra’

Egyptian Cobra: ‘Egyptian’ – it lives in Egypt; ‘Cobra’ – from the Portuguese cobra de capelo, ‘hooded snake’.

Worked out from the Latin and Greek roots and the words in the name; parts SnakeList can’t explain are left out.

Classification notes

Its species name 'haje' comes from the Arabic word for snake. The Snouted Cobra and Anchieta's Cobra used to be counted as kinds of Egyptian Cobra but are now separate species.[1]

SnakeList doesn’t list its older scientific names. The Reptile Database keeps a list of its older scientific names, with who used them and when.
