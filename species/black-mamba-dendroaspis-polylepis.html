Black Mamba

Dendroaspis polylepis Günther, 1864

The Black Mamba is Africa's longest venomous snake: a slender, fast-moving elapid of the savannas and dry woodlands of eastern and southern Africa. Despite its name it is not black on the outside - it gets the name from the inky-black lining of its mouth, which it shows when threatened. Its bite is very dangerous to people without antivenom.[1,2,3]

Identification

Appearance

A long, thin snake with a narrow, coffin-shaped head and smooth scales. The back is olive, khaki, grey-brown or gunmetal grey - rarely truly black - and the belly is paler. The inside of the mouth is blue-black.[1,2]

Size

Typical adults: Adults usually measure 2.0-2.5 m.[1]

Largest records: A maximum of about 4.3 m is accepted; claims of 4.5 m exist but are rare and less certain.[1,5]

Similar species

  • Green Mamba Dendroaspis angusticeps – a relative from the coastal forests of East and southern Africa; adults are bright green and live mainly in trees, while the Black Mamba is grey-brown and lives mainly on the ground.[6,1]

Range

Published distribution

Native: Sub-Saharan Africa: from the Horn of Africa and East Africa south through south-eastern Africa, and west to Namibia and Angola. Records from West Africa are few and scattered.[5,4]

Ecology

Habitat

Mostly a ground-dwelling snake of open country: moist and dry savanna, woodland, rocky hills, riverine forest and coastal bush. It hides in holes in the ground, rock crevices, hollow trees, old termite mounds and under tree stumps, and often returns to the same shelter for a long time.[4,3]

Behaviour

Active by day. It spends much of its time basking near its retreat, and its shelters are sometimes shared with other snakes. It usually escapes when disturbed, but if cornered it rears up, leaning forward, spreads a narrow hood and opens its black mouth as a warning.[4,3,6]

Speed: One of the fastest snakes, though sources disagree on the figure: about 7-10 km/h when travelling, with short bursts estimated at 15-20 km/h.[3]

Diet

Mainly small mammals such as rodents and, in rocky areas, hyraxes (dassies); it also takes birds. It bites its prey and lets the venom work rather than constricting.[7,4]

Reproduction

Lays eggs, which hatch after about two to three months. Clutch size differs between sources: 6-17 eggs in some accounts, 10-25 in others.[1,2,4]

Conservation

LCListed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List: it is widespread and not considered at risk of extinction.[5]
