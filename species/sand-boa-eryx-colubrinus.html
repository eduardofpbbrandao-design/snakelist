Sand Boa

Eryx colubrinus (Linnaeus, 1758)

The East African or Kenyan Sand Boa is a small, thick-bodied, burrowing boa of the dry lands of north-eastern and East Africa. It spends most of its life buried in sand or loose soil, waiting for prey to come within reach. It is also one of the most commonly kept small snakes in captivity.[1,2]

Identification

Appearance

A short, stout snake with a small head that is barely wider than the neck, tiny eyes set high on the head and a short tail. It is usually orange or yellow with irregular dark brown blotches.[1,2]

Size

Wild adults: Females are much larger than males: adult females are rarely more than about 91 cm in total length, and most adults are about 30-60 cm.[1,3]

Newborns: About 20-25 cm long at birth.[1]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: North-eastern and East Africa, from Egypt west to Niger (the Aïr mountains) and south through Sudan, Ethiopia and Somalia to Kenya and northern Tanzania.[1]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Semi-desert, dry scrub savanna and rocky outcrops, wherever there is loose sand or soil to burrow into.[1]

Behaviour

A burrowing ambush hunter: it lies buried with only its head showing and seizes passing prey from below, often at night, before pulling it under the sand.[1,2]

Diet

Kills its prey by constriction. In the wild it eats small vertebrates such as rodents and lizards.[1,2]

Reproduction

Gives birth to live young (some sources describe it as ovoviviparous, with eggs hatching inside the mother).[1]

Captivity: In captivity in the United States it breeds readily from about November to April, and litters average about 10-20 young, born from spring to late summer.[1]

Who described it

First described by Linnaeus in 1758. The brackets around the authority, (Linnaeus, 1758), mean it was first described in a different genus.

Classification notes

Some authors place it in the genus Gongylophis as Gongylophis colubrinus, a name still common in the pet trade; most current lists use Eryx colubrinus. A form from Kenya and Tanzania has been named E. c. loveridgei.[1,2]
