Russell's Viper

Daboia russelii (Shaw & Nodder, 1797)

Russell's Viper is a large, heavy-bodied viper of South Asia and one of India's "Big Four" medically important snakes. It is common in open farmland, where it hunts rats and mice, and this brings it into frequent contact with people working in the fields.[1,2]

Identification

Appearance

A stout viper with a flat, triangular head and a pattern of three rows of large, dark-brown spots, each ringed in black and edged with white or yellow, running the length of the body.[1,3]

Size

Adults: Mainland adults average about 120 cm and reach a maximum of about 166 cm; island populations stay smaller. Some sources give a maximum of about 160 cm.[1,2]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: The Indian subcontinent west of the Bay of Bengal: India, Pakistan, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka. Populations east of the Bay of Bengal are now treated as a separate species, the Eastern Russell's Viper.[1,2]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Mostly open, grassy or bushy country, including plains, coastal lowlands, hills and farmland. It generally avoids dense, damp forest.[1]

Behaviour

When threatened it coils and gives a very loud hiss, said to be louder than that of almost any other snake, before striking.[1]

Diet

Rodents are its main food, especially field and house rats and mice such as Rattus and Bandicota. That is why it is drawn to fields, villages and grain stores.[1,2]

Reproduction

Gives birth to live young. Litters of 20-40 are typical; sources disagree on the record, with maximums of 65 and 75 young both reported.[1]

Who described it

First described by Shaw & Nodder in 1797. The brackets around the authority, (Shaw & Nodder, 1797), mean it was first described in a different genus.

Where the name comes from

Daboia: thought to come from a Hindi word, dabohiya, meaning "that lies hid" or "the lurker"[5]

russelii: named after Patrick Russell, a Scottish doctor who studied the snakes of India

Russell's Viper: ‘Russell's’ – named after Patrick Russell, a Scottish doctor who studied the snakes of India; ‘Viper’ – from the Latin vipera, ‘bearing live young’ - most vipers give birth instead of laying eggs.

Worked out from the Latin and Greek roots and the words in the name; parts SnakeList can’t explain are left out.

Classification notes

Russell's Viper was long treated as one species with several subspecies across South and South-east Asia. Analyses of body features and mitochondrial DNA led to the eastern populations being recognised as a separate species, Daboia siamensis.[1]
