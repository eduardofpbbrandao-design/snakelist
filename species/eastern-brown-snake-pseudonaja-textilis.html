Eastern Brown Snake

Pseudonaja textilis (Duméril, Bibron & Duméril, 1854)

The Eastern Brown Snake is a fast, slender and highly venomous elapid of eastern Australia, with a population in southern New Guinea. It thrives in farmland and on the edges of towns, where introduced mice and rats are plentiful, so it is one of the snakes Australians meet most often.[1,2]

Identification

Appearance

Adults are usually a plain shade of brown, from pale fawn to almost black, with a paler, often cream or yellowish belly that may be marked with orange or grey blotches. Juveniles can be banded and often have a dark head and a dark band across the neck.[1,2]

Its fangs are small for such a dangerous snake: about 2.8 mm on average, and up to 4 mm in large specimens.[1]

Size

Adults: A slender snake that can reach about 2 m in length.[1]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: Eastern Australia, from northern Queensland through New South Wales and Victoria to South Australia, with scattered populations in the Northern Territory, and parts of southern New Guinea.[1,2]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Most habitats except dense forest, including grassland, woodland, scrub and especially farmland and the outskirts of cities.[1]

Behaviour

Active mainly by day. When threatened it may raise the front of its body high off the ground in an S-shape with its mouth open, ready to strike.[1,2]

Diet

In the wild it eats a wide range of vertebrates - frogs, reptiles and reptile eggs, birds and mammals - and especially introduced House Mice and rats.[2]

Reproduction

Females lay eggs.[1]

Conservation

Broad-scale clearing of land for farming, harmful to many native animals, has helped this species by creating open country full of rodents.[2]

Snakebite: It is highly venomous and causes many of Australia's serious snakebites. Bites need urgent medical treatment with antivenom.[1,2]
