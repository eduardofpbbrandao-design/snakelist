Prairie Kingsnake

Lampropeltis calligaster (Harlan, 1827)

The Prairie Kingsnake is a calm, non-venomous kingsnake of the midwestern and southern United States.[1]

Identification

How to recognise it

  • Reddish-brown blotches on a light brown back.[1]
  • Yellowish belly.[1]

Appearance

Light brown or grey-brown with dark reddish-brown blotches down the back and a pale or yellowish belly. It can be mistaken for a rat snake; the markings may fade with age.[1]

Size

Adults: About 76 to 110 cm; males are longer and heavier on average.[1]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: The midwestern and southern USA, from Iowa, Illinois and Indiana south to Texas, Louisiana and Alabama.[1,2]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Prairie, pasture and shrubland, and the edges of grassland, roads and woods; seldom forest, and never cropland.[1]

Behaviour

Usually gentle and slow to bite; if bothered it shakes its tail (which can sound like a rattle in dry leaves) and gives off a smelly musk. In Illinois it hibernates from mid-October to late March.[1]

Diet

Kills by squeezing (constriction). Prairie voles are its most common prey, with other small mammals, reptiles and quail eggs.[1]

Reproduction

Lays about 10 eggs in June or July, which hatch in late August or September. Young often double their length in the first year.[1]

Who described it

First described by Harlan in 1827. The brackets around the authority, (Harlan, 1827), mean it was first described in a different genus.

Where the name comes from

Lampropeltis: from the Greek lampros (shiny) and peltē (small shield) - for the smooth, glossy scales[3]

calligaster: ‘beautiful belly’

Prairie Kingsnake: ‘Kingsnake’ – it eats other snakes, even venomous ones.
