Western Diamond-backed Rattlesnake

Crotalus atrox Baird & Girard, 1853

The Western Diamond-backed Rattlesnake is a large, heavy pit viper of the dry south-western United States and Mexico. It is one of the best-known rattlesnakes in North America, recognised by the diamond pattern on its back and the bold black-and-white rings just in front of its rattle. Its venom is dangerous to people: it is thought to cause most snakebite deaths in northern Mexico.[1,2]

Identification

Appearance

A thick-bodied rattlesnake with a broad, triangular head and heat-sensing pits between eye and nostril. The back is grey, brown or pinkish, marked with darker, light-edged diamonds, and the scales are speckled with dark spots, giving a dusty look.[2,5]

The tail just before the rattle has black and white rings of about the same width - the reason for its nickname, coon tail.[1,2,5]

Size

Typical adults: Adults commonly reach about 120 cm; snakes over 150 cm are uncommon.[1]

Largest record: The largest reliably measured specimen was about 213 cm long.[1]

Newborns: Babies are about 30 cm long at birth.[3]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: The south-western United States - Texas, Oklahoma, New Mexico and Arizona, reaching southern California and Nevada - and much of northern and central Mexico.[1,2]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Very adaptable. It lives in deserts, grasslands, scrubland, rocky canyons and even pine-oak forest, from coastal lowlands up to about 2,100 m (7,000 ft). It shelters in rock crevices and in burrows dug by mammals.[2,6]

Behaviour

Lives on the ground. It is more active by day in spring and autumn, and mostly at dusk and at night in the heat of summer. In the cooler parts of its range it is usually inactive from late autumn to early spring.[1,7]

Diet

Mostly small mammals such as rodents and rabbits; it also eats birds, lizards and amphibians. Young snakes eat more lizards than adults do.[1,2]

Reproduction

Gives birth to live young instead of laying eggs. Litters of up to about 25 babies have been reported, and the young are on their own soon after birth. Females mature at around three years old.[2,3]

Did you know?

  • Its nickname coon tail comes from the black-and-white rings on its tail, which look like a raccoon's.[1,2]
  • A rattlesnake adds a new segment to its rattle each time it sheds its skin - but segments also break off, so you can't count them to find its age.[8]

Conservation

LC

Least Concern on the IUCN Red List[1,4]

Global assessment · assessed 2007 · IUCN assessment

Listed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List, thanks to its very wide range and large population.

Not threatened overall and locally common. It is still collected in large numbers at rattlesnake round-ups in Texas, which have been criticised by wildlife groups.[4,9]

Similar and related snakes

Similar species and how to tell them apart

  • Mojave Rattlesnake Crotalus scutulatus – shares much of its range. The Mojave usually has white tail rings wider than the black ones (about 2:1), two or three large scales between the scales above the eyes instead of several small ones, and a pale stripe behind the eye that runs past the corner of the mouth. Both species vary, so check several features together.[5,10,11]
