Iberian Grass Snake

Natrix astreptophora (Seoane, 1884)

The Iberian Grass Snake, also called the Red-eyed Grass Snake, is the grass snake of Portugal, Spain, southern France and the coast of north-western Africa. For a long time it was treated as part of the common Grass Snake, but in 2016 scientists showed it is a separate species. It is not venomous to people and lives near water, where it hunts fish and frogs.[1,2]

Identification

Appearance

Its most reliable feature is the reddish colour of the iris, which sets it apart from all other grass snakes. Compared with them it also has fewer belly scales and a differently shaped skull - details that need a specimen in the hand.[2,4]

Size

Adults: Females grow much larger than males: about 90-120 cm, against about 55-85 cm for males.[3]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: The Iberian Peninsula (Portugal and Spain), neighbouring southern France, and scattered coastal areas of north-western Africa, in Morocco, Algeria and Tunisia. The snakes of Algeria and Tunisia are treated as a separate subspecies, N. a. algerica, and those of northern Morocco were described in 2025 as N. a. soumiafahdae. In North Africa it is rare and lives in small, separate patches.[1,5,6]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Slow-flowing and still fresh water - streams, rivers, ponds and their banks - usually in the shallows. In Iberian rivers it is more numerous where there are no invasive fish.[1,3]

Diet

Mainly fish and amphibians; small mammals and young birds are also eaten.[1]

Reproduction

Lays eggs. Sources disagree on clutch size: young females are thought to lay around ten eggs, while large, older females may lay many more (figures of 50 and even 100 are given).[1,3]

Did you know?

  • Scientists only recognised this snake as its own species in 2016 - it had been hiding in plain sight as a kind of grass snake.[2]

Conservation

Sources disagree on its global status: one lists it as Least Concern in the European and global IUCN Red Lists, another as not yet evaluated. Andorra's national Red List rates it Vulnerable there.[1,3,7]

Who described it

First described by Seoane in 1884. The brackets around the authority, (Seoane, 1884), mean it was first described in a different genus.

What the name means

Natrix: the classical Latin word for a water snake, related to the German Natter and English adder[8]

Classification notes

Described by Seoane in the 1880s, then treated for over a century as a subspecies of the Grass Snake (Natrix natrix astreptophora). A 2016 study of its body shape, skull and DNA recognised it as a full species. Older field guides therefore call it a grass snake.[2,4]

Fossils of this snake from Spain date from the late Pliocene to the late Pleistocene.[1]
