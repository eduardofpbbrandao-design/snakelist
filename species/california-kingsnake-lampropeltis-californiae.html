California Kingsnake

Lampropeltis californiae (Blainville, 1835)

The California Kingsnake is a medium-sized, non-venomous constrictor of the western United States and north-western Mexico. It is one of the most popular pet snakes, and escaped or released animals have founded an invasive population on Gran Canaria in the Canary Islands, where it is badly harming native reptiles.[1,2]

Identification

Appearance

Most wild animals are banded, with alternating dark brown or black and white or cream rings. Some populations include striped animals with a pale stripe running along the back, and the pet trade has produced many other colour forms, including albinos.[1]

Size

Adults: Mean adult length is about 76-122 cm, with a maximum of about 200 cm.[3]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: From south-western Oregon south through most of California to the tip of Baja California, and east into Nevada, southern Utah, western Arizona and Sonora.[1,3]

Introduced: Gran Canaria, Canary Islands (Spain). Introduced through the pet trade, it was found in the wild in the late 1990s (1998 in several studies) and was considered naturalised by 2007.[2,5]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Very adaptable: woodland, chaparral, grassland, desert, farmland and suburban areas, often near rocks or streams.[1,4]

Diet

Kills prey by constriction. It eats rodents, lizards, birds and their eggs, and other snakes; it has been photographed eating rattlesnakes in the wild. A 2019 study of stomach contents from museum specimens examined its diet across its range.[1,4,6]

Reproduction

Lays eggs.[1]

Gran Canaria: Regional: on Gran Canaria, studies have found fewer juveniles but adults that are longer and heavier than in the native range.[7]

Did you know?

  • The first kingsnakes found on Gran Canaria included albinos - a sign that they came from captive-bred pets.[8]

Conservation

As an invader: On Gran Canaria, sites invaded by kingsnakes had far fewer native reptiles: more than 90% fewer Gran Canaria Giant Lizards, more than 80% fewer Gran Canaria Skinks and more than 50% fewer Boettger's Wall Geckos. EU-funded LIFE projects have worked to control it.[2,5]

Who described it

First described by Blainville in 1835. The brackets around the authority, (Blainville, 1835), mean it was first described in a different genus.

What the name means

Lampropeltis: from the Greek lampros (shiny) and peltē (small shield) - for the smooth, glossy scales[9]

Classification notes

Long treated as the subspecies Lampropeltis getula californiae, it was raised to a full species in 2009, when Pyron and Burbrink split the Common Kingsnake into five species. Not all authorities follow this.[1,3]
