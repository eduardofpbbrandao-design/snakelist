Saw-scaled Viper

Echis carinatus (Schneider, 1801)

The Saw-scaled Viper is a small but dangerous viper of dry country from the Middle East and Central Asia to India and Sri Lanka. It is the smallest member of India's "Big Four" - the four species responsible for most serious snakebites there - and it causes a large share of those bites.[1,2]

Identification

Appearance

A short, stocky viper with a pear-shaped head, large eyes and rough, keeled scales. The scales along the flanks are angled and serrated, and the snake rubs them together to make its warning sound.[1,2]

Colour varies with the ground it lives on, usually shades of brown, grey or sand with a row of pale blotches or a wavy pale line along each side.[1]

Size

Adults: Between about 38 and 80 cm in total length, and usually less than 60 cm. Some popular sources give larger figures, up to about 90 cm.[1,2]

Variation

Subspecies: Several subspecies have been named. E. c. sochureki is listed from Afghanistan, Pakistan, northern India, Iran and the Arabian Gulf coast (Oman and the UAE), and E. c. multisquamatus from Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan to Iran and western Pakistan. Sources disagree on how many subspecies to recognise.[1]

Similar species

  • African Saw-scaled Viper Echis ocellatus – an African relative from West Africa; it looks much alike, and the two ranges do not meet.[4]
  • Egyptian Saw-scaled Viper Echis pyramidum – the Egyptian Saw-scaled Viper of north-east Africa and Arabia, another closely related, similar-looking species.[4]

Ecology

Habitat

Dry open places on a wide range of ground: sand, rock, soft soil and scrubland. It often hides under loose stones, and is sometimes found climbing in bushes up to about 2 m above the ground.[1]

Behaviour

Mostly active at night. When threatened it forms C-shaped coils and rubs its serrated side scales against each other, producing a rasping, sizzling sound as a warning before striking.[1,2]

Reproduction

India: Regional: the Indian population gives birth to live young. In northern India mating takes place in winter and young are born from about April to August.[1]

Elsewhere: Some sources report that E. c. sochureki lays eggs; this has not been confirmed here by a second source.[1]

Conservation

Snakebite: It is one of the most medically important snakes in South Asia. Estimates of how many deaths it causes each year vary widely between sources, so no single figure is given here.[2,1]
