Bushmaster

Lachesis muta (Linnaeus, 1766)

The South American Bushmaster is the longest pit viper in the Americas and is often called the longest viper in the world. It lives in the rainforests of the Amazon basin and in Brazil's Atlantic Forest, and it is unusual among New World pit vipers in laying eggs.[1,2,3]

Identification

Appearance

A long, fairly slender pit viper with rough, strongly keeled scales. It is tan, yellowish or reddish-brown with a row of dark diamond-shaped blotches along the back, and the tail ends in a short spine of modified scales.[1,3]

Size

Adults: Most adults exceed 2 m, and lengths over 3.6 m are rare. Published maximums vary between sources, from about 3.4 to 3.6 m.[2,3,4]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: Tropical South America east of the Andes, mainly the Amazon basin and the Guianas, and Trinidad. A separate population lives in the Atlantic Forest of eastern Brazil.[1,3]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Lowland tropical rainforest, where it spends much of its time on the forest floor.[1,4]

Behaviour

Mostly nocturnal and an ambush hunter that waits coiled for prey to pass.[1]

Diet

Feeds mainly on rodents and other small mammals.[1,4]

Reproduction

Females lay eggs rather than giving birth, which sets bushmasters apart from almost all other pit vipers of the Americas. Clutch sizes reported range from about 5 to 18 eggs, and incubation lasts roughly two months.[1,3,4]

Captivity: Captive females have been seen coiling around and attending their clutch.[1]

Did you know?

  • Bushmasters are the only pit vipers in the Americas known for certain to lay eggs; nearly all their relatives give birth to live young.[3,4]

Who described it

First described by Linnaeus in 1766. The brackets around the authority, (Linnaeus, 1766), mean it was first described in a different genus.

What the name means

Lachesis: probably named after Lachesis, one of the three Fates of Greek myth, who measured out the length of each life[6]

Classification notes

The Atlantic Forest population has traditionally been the subspecies L. m. rhombeata. A 2024 study by Hamdan and colleagues treated it as a full species, Lachesis rhombeata, but not every source has adopted this yet.[7,8]
