Western Green Mamba

Dendroaspis viridis (Hallowell, 1844)

The Western Green Mamba is a long, slender, highly venomous mamba of the coastal forests of West Africa. It spends most of its life in trees, but comes down to the ground more often than its eastern relative.[1,2]

Identification

Appearance

Bright green above with yellowish tones. Its scales are rimmed in black, which gives the body a fine net-like pattern.[1]

Scale counts help confirm it: it has only 13 rows of long, narrow scales around the middle of the body - fewer than similar species - and 211-225 belly scales.[1]

Size

Adults: Most adults are between about 1.4 and 2.1 m long, and large individuals approach 2.4 m. The African Snakebite Institute gives an average of about 1.8 m and a maximum near 2.3 m; some websites claim over 3 m, which is not well supported.[1,2]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: West Africa, from the Gambia and southern Senegal east to Benin. In Togo it reaches into drier open forest and Guinea savanna as well as the coast. An isolated record from the Central African Republic is sometimes mentioned.[1]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Mainly coastal tropical rainforest, thicket and woodland.[1]

Behaviour

Active by day and mostly in the canopy, sometimes high above the ground. It is usually nervous and quick to escape; when cornered it may hiss loudly and strike repeatedly.[1]

Diet

Mainly birds and small mammals such as rodents and squirrels; it may take birds' eggs and occasionally lizards.[1]

Reproduction

Females lay eggs. Reported clutch sizes are about 6-14, but this figure comes from secondary sources and is not well documented.[1]

Conservation

Snakebite: Its venom is dangerous, but bites are poorly documented, and the number of serious bites it causes each year is not known.[1]

Similar species and how to tell them apart

Who described it

First described by Hallowell in 1844. The brackets around the authority, (Hallowell, 1844), mean it was first described in a different genus.

What the name means

Dendroaspis: from the Greek dendron (tree) and aspis (asp, an old word for a cobra or snake) - "tree asp", as most mambas live in trees[3]
