Burmese Python

Python bivittatus Kuhl, 1820

The Burmese Python is one of the largest snakes in the world: a heavy-bodied, non-venomous constrictor from the lowlands of South and Southeast Asia. It is at home both on land and in water. People have also brought it to southern Florida, where escaped or released pets founded a large wild population that now eats native mammals, birds and even alligators.[1,2]

Identification

Appearance

A thick, muscular python with smooth scales. The body is light brown to yellowish tan, covered in large dark reddish-brown blotches edged in black that fit together like puzzle pieces or a giraffe's pattern.[7,8]

On top of the head there is a dark wedge or arrowhead mark, and further dark wedges sit behind the eye and below the head.[7]

Size

Typical adults: Wild Burmese Pythons average about 3.7 m (12 ft) long.[1]

Largest records: Individuals of up to about 5.8 m (19 ft) have been reported. The largest python caught in Florida up to 2022 was a 5.36 m (17 ft 7 in) female weighing 74.8 kg (164.5 lb), now kept at the Florida Museum of Natural History.[1,9]

Variation

Age: Young pythons climb well and use their grasping tails in trees; as they grow heavier, adults spend most of their time on the ground.[4]

Similar species

  • Indian Rock Python Python molurus – its closest relative, usually paler and greyer overall, with a less distinct mark on top of the head in adults. Colour varies, so head markings and scales around the eye are checked together.[1]
  • African Rock Python Python sebae – also found as an introduced snake in Florida; it has a more broken, irregular pattern and a different head pattern, and Florida's wildlife agency gives separate identification guides for the two.[7]

Range

Published distribution

Native: Tropical South and Southeast Asia, including Myanmar, Thailand, Vietnam and Indonesia. SnakeList's map below shows the countries where it is recorded.[1,4]

Introduced: Southern Florida, USA. Pythons have been found there since at least 1979, and the population was probably breeding and established by the 1980s. It is centred on the Everglades and has spread across much of southern Florida.[2]

Ecology

Habitat

A habitat generalist. In its native range it lives in rainforest, swamps, grasslands and marshes. In southern Florida the Everglades suit it well: a warm climate, a mix of trees, brush and marsh, plenty of prey and few large predators.[4,2,10]

Behaviour

Mostly active at night and spends much of the day hidden in vegetation. It is an excellent swimmer and can stay under water for around half an hour.[4]

Diet

Kills its prey by constriction and eats mainly mammals and birds. In Florida it has been recorded eating animals from small rodents up to White-tailed Deer and American Alligators, and scientists say it is changing food webs across southern Florida.[2,10]

Reproduction

Females lay eggs, usually in spring. They coil around the clutch and twitch their muscles to warm the eggs, staying until they hatch about two to three months later. The young get no care after hatching.[4,1]

Florida clutches: Nests studied in Florida held 22 to 84 eggs (49 on average). The largest wild clutch documented, found in 2023, held 96 eggs.[3]

Conservation

VUListed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List (global assessment by Stuart and colleagues, 2012), reflecting declines in its native range.[1,5]

In its native range it is threatened by habitat loss and by being caught for its skin, meat, traditional medicine and the live pet trade. It is listed in CITES Appendix II, which controls international trade.[5,1]

In Florida: The situation is reversed: there it is an invasive species, and state and federal agencies run removal programmes to protect native wildlife.[2,7]
