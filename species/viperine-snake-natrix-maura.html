Viperine Snake

Natrix maura (Linnaeus, 1758)

The Viperine Snake is a small water snake of south-western Europe and north-western Africa. It spends much of its life in and around rivers, ponds and streams, hunting fish and frogs. Its zigzag pattern, a head that can look triangular and a viper-like defensive display make many people mistake it for a viper - which is how it got its common name - but it has no venom dangerous to people.[1,2]

Identification

Appearance

Grey, brown or reddish above, with a dark zigzag stripe along the middle of the back and a row of dark eye-spots with yellowish centres along each side. Viper-like markings and a head that can look triangular are the reason it is confused with vipers.[1]

Size

Adults: A relatively small snake, reaching up to about 80 cm in total length; a typical adult length is not given in SnakeList's sources.[2]

Males and females: Females are the larger sex: in studies in Sardinia and in the Ebro Delta (Spain), females were longer than males.[4,5]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: Portugal, Spain (including Gibraltar), France, south-western Switzerland and north-western Italy, plus Sardinia; and in North Africa, Morocco, northern Algeria, Tunisia and north-western Libya.[1,3]

Central Algeria: A 2024 record from central Algeria extended its known range southwards, into the Sahara.[6]

Introduced: Mallorca and Menorca in the Balearic Islands. A genetic study suggests it reached Mallorca from south-eastern France around Roman times, so it is an ancient introduction rather than a recent one.[7]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Closely tied to fresh water: rivers, streams, ponds, lakes, irrigation channels and other wetlands, where it hunts in the water and basks on the banks.[1,2]

Behaviour

When threatened on land it hisses and strikes in a viper-like way, but usually does not actually bite.[1]

Diet

Mainly fish and amphibians, caught in the water.[1,2]

Young snakes (Ebro Delta, Spain): Juveniles ate a more varied diet than adults, taking many tadpoles and earthworms; adults ate mostly fish. Males took more small carp and large females more frogs.[5]

On Mallorca: It preys on Perez's Frog, among other animals.[1]

Reproduction

Lays eggs. In one study, females did not lay bigger or more frequent clutches when food was more plentiful, unlike many other snakes.[1,4]

Conservation

LC

Least Concern on the IUCN Red List[3]

Global assessment · assessment year not recorded

Listed as Least Concern globally, although its numbers are reported to be declining.[1]

On Mallorca: Predation by viperine snakes is believed to be one of the causes of the decline of the rare Mallorcan Midwife Toad, and removing snakes has been tried at some toad sites.[8,9]

Who described it

First described by Linnaeus in 1758. The brackets around the authority, (Linnaeus, 1758), mean it was first described in a different genus.

What the name means

Natrix: the classical Latin word for a water snake, related to the German Natter and English adder[11]
