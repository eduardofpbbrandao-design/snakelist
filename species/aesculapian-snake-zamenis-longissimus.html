Aesculapian Snake

Zamenis longissimus (Laurenti, 1768)

The Aesculapian Snake is one of Europe's largest snakes and an excellent climber. It is named after Asclepius (Aesculapius), the Greek and Roman god of medicine: the snake coiled around his staff, still used in medical logos today, is thought to be this species.[1]

Identification

Appearance

Adults are light brown to bronze with smooth scales that give a metallic sheen, sometimes with small white dots. The belly scales are angled sharply at the sides, which helps it climb.[1,3]

Variation

Young: Juveniles are light green or brownish-green with darker markings along the back and sides.[1]

Sexes: Adult males grow larger than females.[1]

Size

Adults: Usually not longer than about 1.6 m; males grow larger than females and may reach just over 2 m.[1]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: From eastern France across central and southern Europe to the Balkans and the Black Sea region, and south into northern Italy. A closely related Italian form has been raised to a separate species, the Italian Aesculapian Snake (Zamenis lineatus).[1,4]

Introduced: Small introduced populations exist in Britain.[2,5]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Warm, moderately humid, hilly or rocky forested places; it avoids open plains and intensively farmed land.[1]

Behaviour

A good climber, often seen in bushes and trees, where it raids bird nests.[1,2]

Diet

Mainly small mammals - shrews, moles and rodents up to the size of rats - plus birds, their eggs and nestlings.[1,2]

Did you know?

  • The snake on the medical symbol, the Rod of Asclepius, is believed to be an Aesculapian Snake.[1]

Who described it

First described by Laurenti in 1768. The brackets around the authority, (Laurenti, 1768), mean it was first described in a different genus.

Where the name comes from

Zamenis: from the Greek zamenes, meaning angry, irritable or fierce[6]

longissimus: ‘very long’

Worked out from the Latin and Greek roots and the words in the name; parts SnakeList can’t explain are left out.
