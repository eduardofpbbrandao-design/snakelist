Black Rat Snake

Pantherophis obsoletus (Say, 1823)

The Western Rat Snake (often called the Black Rat Snake or Texas Rat Snake) is a large, non-venomous constrictor of the central United States west of the Mississippi River. It climbs extremely well and is often found around barns and farm buildings, where it eats rodents.[1,2]

Identification

Appearance

Adults vary from mostly black, with traces of a blotched pattern, to brownish or yellowish with dark blotches, depending on the region. Young snakes are boldly blotched grey and brown.[1,2]

Size

Adults: Missouri's Department of Conservation gives 107-183 cm (3½-6 ft) for adults, occasionally to 218 cm (86 in).[1]

Similar species

  • Gray Rat Snake Pantherophis spiloides – the Gray Rat Snake, its neighbour east of the Mississippi, which keeps a grey blotched pattern as an adult.[4]
  • Great Plains Rat Snake Pantherophis emoryi – the Great Plains Rat Snake, a smaller relative of the plains that keeps a blotched pattern as an adult.[5]

Ecology

Habitat

Wooded valleys and bluffs, woodland edges, rocky hillsides, farmland and old fields, often near buildings.[1,2]

Behaviour

An agile climber that hunts actively and kills its prey by constriction. Rat snakes are sometimes seen perched on branches near cave mouths, trying to catch bats as they fly out.[3,6]

Diet

Constricts its prey. It eats rats, mice and other small mammals, birds and their eggs, lizards and frogs. One snake was recorded eating a whole clutch of Mallard eggs.[3,1]

Reproduction

Females lay eggs in summer. Animal Diversity Web gives an average clutch of about 15 eggs, ranging from 4 to 44; other sources give narrower ranges of about 12-20.[3]

Pantherophis obsoletus (Say, 1823)

Since Burbrink's 2001 study, the old Rat Snake has usually been split into three species, with P. obsoletus kept for the western lineage. The split rests mainly on mitochondrial DNA, and some herpetologists, including Hillis (2022), have questioned whether these lineages should be full species.[6,7]
