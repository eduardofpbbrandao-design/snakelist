Habitats from reviewed species accounts

What SnakeList’s reviewed, sourced accounts say about where each species lives in general, across its range. They do not describe Arkansas in particular. The other 14 species here have no reviewed account yet, so nothing is said about their habitats.

  • Black Rat Snake Pantherophis obsoletus: Wooded valleys and bluffs, woodland edges, rocky hillsides, farmland and old fields, often near buildings. Source: Missouri Department of Conservation, Louisiana Department of Wildlife and Fisheries.
  • Brown Snake Storeria dekayi: Most kinds of wet and dry habitats east of the Great Plains, from sea level to about 1,400 m. It likes moist places and is most often found near people - in cities more than in the countryside. Source: Wikipedia.
  • Coachwhip Masticophis flagellum: Open, sandy places: pine woods, old fields, prairies, sandhill scrub and coastal dunes. Source: Wikipedia.
  • Common Gartersnake Thamnophis sirtalis: Usually close to water: along rivers, lakes and reservoirs, in wetlands and in the strips of plants beside streams. It also turns up at forest edges and even in vacant lots in towns, but is rarely found in dry places far from water. Source: South Dakota Amphibians and Reptiles, Illinois Natural History Survey.
  • Common Wormsnake Carphophis amoenus: Moist, rocky woodland, under rotting logs and stumps. Source: Wikipedia.
  • Copperhead Agkistrodon contortrix: In many areas it lives in high, dry, rocky, well-wooded places dominated by oaks and hickories. Source: Indiana Herp Atlas.
  • Cottonmouth Agkistrodon piscivorus: Almost any habitat with water: swamps, marshes, slow rivers and streams, lake and pond edges, ditches and bottomland forest. Studied home ranges were small, around one hectare, and usually included a stream or lake. Source: Animal Diversity Web, University of Michigan, Tennessee Wildlife Resources Agency.
  • Diamondback Water Snake Nerodia rhombifer: Near slow-moving water: streams, rivers, ponds and swamps. Source: Wikipedia.
  • Eastern Hognose Snake Heterodon platirhinos: Open areas with sandy, loose soils. Loose soil is important for nesting and egg-laying, and for digging out prey. Source: Government of Canada, Species at Risk Public Registry, Wikipedia.
  • Graham's Crayfish Snake Regina grahamii: Edges of muddy marshes, oxbow lakes, rivers, streams and roadside ditches full of crayfish; it hides under debris and in crayfish burrows. Source: Wikipedia.
  • Lined Snake Tropidoclonion lineatum: Savanna, shrubland and grassland with soft, moist soil - also backyards, vacant lots and dumps. Source: Wikipedia.
  • Milk Snake Lampropeltis triangulum: Rocky hillsides, open woodland, woodland edges, clearings and old fields, and also farm buildings and rock outcrops. It spends winter in rock crevices, mammal burrows or building foundations. Source: The Canadian Encyclopedia, Maine Department of Inland Fisheries and Wildlife.
  • Prairie Kingsnake Lampropeltis calligaster: Prairie, pasture and shrubland, and the edges of grassland, roads and woods; seldom forest, and never cropland. Source: Wikipedia.
  • Queen Snake Regina septemvittata: Only clean running streams with stony or rocky bottoms, where the water stays at least about 10 °C while it is active. Source: Wikipedia.
  • Racer Coluber constrictor: Usually open, grassy places, but also brush, swamps, roadsides and suburbs; in Florida it is the commonest snake in neighbourhoods. Source: Wikipedia.
  • Ringneck Snake Diadophis punctatus: Many habitats, wherever there is plenty of cover: open woodland near rocky hillsides, forests, damp places with fallen wood, and streamside areas in dry regions. Source: Wikipedia.
  • Southern Water Snake Nerodia fasciata: Almost any fresh water - lakes, marshes, ponds and streams - staying among plants along the shore. Source: Wikipedia.
  • Speckled Kingsnake Lampropeltis holbrooki: Prefers wetter places than other kingsnakes, such as swamps and rivers, but also uses woodland and grassy fields. Source: Wikipedia.
  • Western Diamond-backed Rattlesnake Crotalus atrox: Very adaptable. It lives in deserts, grasslands, scrubland, rocky canyons and even pine-oak forest, from coastal lowlands up to about 2,100 m (7,000 ft). It shelters in rock crevices and in burrows dug by mammals. Source: Animal Diversity Web, University of Michigan, Pajarito Environmental Education Center (PEEC) Nature.
  • Western Ribbonsnake Thamnophis proximus: Many habitats, usually not far from water, from sea level to nearly 2,500 m. Source: Wikipedia.
