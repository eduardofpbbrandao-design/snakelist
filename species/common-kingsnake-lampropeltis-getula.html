Common Kingsnake

Lampropeltis getula (Linnaeus, 1766)

The Common or Eastern Kingsnake is a glossy, powerful, non-venomous constrictor of the eastern United States, famous for eating other snakes - including venomous ones such as rattlesnakes, copperheads and cottonmouths, whose venom it appears to resist.[1,2]

Identification

Appearance

Shiny black or dark brown with narrow white or yellow crossbands that split along the sides and link up, giving a chain-like pattern - hence the old name Chain Kingsnake. The amount of pale colour varies from place to place.[1,2]

Size

Typical adults: About 90-122 cm (36-48 in) in total length.[1]

Largest: The largest member of its genus, with a maximum recorded total length of 208 cm.[3]

Distribution

Published distribution

In the narrow sense: As defined by Pyron and Burbrink in 2009, the Eastern Kingsnake ranges from New Jersey south to the Florida Keys and west to the Florida panhandle and south-eastern Alabama.[3]

In the broad sense: Many books and some herpetological societies still use the name L. getula for the whole group, which spans much of the United States and northern Mexico. SnakeList's map reflects records under this name.[4]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Uses a wide range of habitats, from pine woods and hardwood forest to the edges of swamps, fields and farmland.[1,2]

Diet

A generalist that kills by constriction. Besides snakes, it eats small mammals, birds and their eggs, frogs, lizards, and turtles and their eggs.[1]

Reproduction

Lays eggs.[2]

Did you know?

  • The "king" in its name refers to its habit of eating other snakes - even venomous ones, whose venom it appears to shrug off.[1,2]

Who described it

First described by Linnaeus in 1766. The brackets around the authority, (Linnaeus, 1766), mean it was first described in a different genus.

What the name means

Lampropeltis: from the Greek lampros (shiny) and peltē (small shield) - for the smooth, glossy scales[7]

Classification notes

In 2009 Pyron and Burbrink split the traditional Common Kingsnake into five species: L. getula (east), L. nigra (Mississippi valley), L. holbrooki (central), L. splendida (desert) and L. californiae (west). The eastern species includes the former subspecies getula, floridana and meansi.[3,8]

Not everyone accepts the split: the Southwestern Herpetologists Society, for example, keeps the traditional single species, arguing the split rests mostly on mitochondrial DNA.[4]

SnakeList doesn’t list its older scientific names. The Reptile Database keeps a list of its older scientific names, with who used them and when.
