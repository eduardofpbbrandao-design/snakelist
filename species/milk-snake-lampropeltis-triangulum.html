Milk Snake

Lampropeltis triangulum (Lacépède, 1789)

The Milk Snake - in its current, narrower sense the Eastern Milksnake - is a medium-sized, non-venomous constrictor of eastern North America, from southern Ontario and Quebec through the north-eastern and central United States. Its name comes from an old myth that it sneaks into barns to drink milk from cows; in fact it visits barns because they are full of rodents.[1,2]

Identification

How to recognise it

  • Greyish or tan, with large, dark-edged reddish-brown blotches along the back and smaller ones on the sides.[3,4]
  • Often a pale Y- or V-shaped mark on the back of the head.[3,4]

Appearance

Greyish or tan with large, dark-edged reddish-brown blotches along the back and smaller blotches on the sides. There is often a pale Y- or V-shaped mark on the back of the head.[3,4]

Size

Adults: Medium-sized: Maine's wildlife agency gives about 60-90 cm (24-36 in). Most are under a metre, though individuals over 130 cm are known.[1,3]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: Southern Ontario and Quebec in Canada and much of the north-eastern and central United States. Where its range meets that of related milksnakes to the west and south, the boundaries between species are still debated.[1,2]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Rocky hillsides, open woodland, woodland edges, clearings and old fields, and also farm buildings and rock outcrops. It spends winter in rock crevices, mammal burrows or building foundations.[1,3]

Diet

Adults eat mainly small mammals, as well as birds, snakes and lizards; young milksnakes feed largely on the young of other snakes. Prey is killed by constriction, though eggs and very small mice may be swallowed alive.[1,2]

Reproduction

Females usually lay one clutch a year in June or July, in rotting logs, leaf piles, burrows, compost or under rocks. Reported clutch sizes differ by region: about 8-16 eggs in Canada, and 3-24 (usually five to seven) in Kansas. The eggs hatch after about two months.[1,2]

Did you know?

  • No snake drinks milk from cows. Milksnakes were simply found in barns, where they hunt the mice and rats that gather there.[1]

Who described it

First described by Lacépède in 1789. The brackets around the authority, (Lacépède, 1789), mean it was first described in a different genus.

Where the name comes from

Lampropeltis: from the Greek lampros (shiny) and peltē (small shield) - for the smooth, glossy scales[6]

triangulum: ‘triangle’

Milk Snake: ‘Milk Snake’ – after an old myth that it drank milk from cows.

Worked out from the Latin and Greek roots and the words in the name; parts SnakeList can’t explain are left out.

Classification notes

The milksnake used to be treated as one species with many subspecies ranging from Canada to South America. In 2014 Ruane and colleagues split it into several species using genetic data, leaving L. triangulum for the eastern populations. Chambers and Hillis (2019) reanalysed the data and disagreed with parts of the split, and the debate continues.[1,2]
