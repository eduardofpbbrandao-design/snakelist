Cottonmouth

Agkistrodon piscivorus (Lacépède, 1789)

The Cottonmouth, also called the Water Moccasin, is a heavy-bodied pit viper of the south-eastern United States and the most aquatic venomous snake in North America. Its common name comes from the white lining of its mouth, which it shows when threatened.[1,2]

Identification

Appearance

Thick-bodied with a broad head and a heat-sensing pit between eye and nostril. Young animals have bold dark crossbands and a bright yellowish tail tip; adults darken with age and many become almost uniformly dark olive, brown or black.[2,3]

When disturbed it often holds its ground, coils and gapes widely to show the white inside of the mouth.[2,3]

Size

Adults: Most adults are roughly 60 cm to 1.2 m (2-4 ft) long. The largest recorded specimen was about 188 cm (74 in).[1,3]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: The south-eastern United States, from south-eastern Virginia west to Missouri and Texas and south to the Florida Keys. Since 2015 the Florida peninsula population is often treated as a separate species, the Florida Cottonmouth.[1,4]

Behaviour and ecology

Habitat

Almost any habitat with water: swamps, marshes, slow rivers and streams, lake and pond edges, ditches and bottomland forest. Studied home ranges were small, around one hectare, and usually included a stream or lake.[1,3]

Behaviour

Swims with its body riding high on the surface, unlike most non-venomous water snakes. It is reluctant to flee and relies on its gaping threat display, but bites mostly happen when it is stepped on or handled.[2,3]

Diet

A broad generalist. Fish and frogs are the main prey, but it also eats small mammals, birds, other snakes, lizards and turtles, and will take carrion.[1,2,3]

Reproduction

Females carry developing young internally for about five months and give birth to live young, usually about 5-9 per litter and up to around 16. Only a small share of each litter is thought to survive to adulthood.[1]

Did you know?

  • Its name comes from the startling cotton-white lining of the mouth that it flashes as a warning.[1,2]

Who described it

First described by Lacépède in 1789. The brackets around the authority, (Lacépède, 1789), mean it was first described in a different genus.

What the name means

Agkistrodon: from the Greek ankistron (fishhook) and odon (tooth), probably for the curved fangs[5]

Classification notes

In 2015 Burbrink and Guiher used genetic data to treat Florida Cottonmouths as a separate species, Agkistrodon conanti, and merged the former northern subspecies into A. piscivorus. The two meet in a zone where they hybridise, and some herpetologists, notably David Hillis, argue they should still be subspecies.[4,6]
