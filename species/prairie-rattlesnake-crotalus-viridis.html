Prairie Rattlesnake

Crotalus viridis (Rafinesque, 1818)

The Prairie Rattlesnake is a venomous pit viper of the Great Plains of North America, from south-western Canada to northern Mexico.[1]

Identification

How to recognise it

  • Rattle on the tail tip.[1]

Appearance

Usually light brown with dark brown blotches down the back. It normally has three or more (usually four) scales between the nostrils on top of the snout.[1]

Size

Adults: Commonly over 100 cm long; the largest recorded was 151.5 cm. The biggest ones are found in Montana.[1]

Distribution

Published distribution

Native: The Great Plains and Rocky Mountain foothills: southern Alberta and Saskatchewan in Canada, much of the western USA from Montana and the Dakotas to Texas and New Mexico, and northern Mexico.[1,2]

Behaviour

Lives mostly on the ground but sometimes climbs into bushes or rests in crevices and caves. Active by day in cool weather and at night in hot weather. Like other rattlesnakes it buzzes its rattle to warn intruders.[1]

Diet

Mostly small mammals - ground squirrels, mice, rats, small rabbits and prairie dogs - and ground-nesting birds; young ones also eat lizards, frogs and snakes.[1]

Reproduction

Gives birth to live young in late summer or early autumn - usually 4 to 12, but from 1 to 25. Females often do not breed every year.[1]

Who described it

First described by Rafinesque in 1818. The brackets around the authority, (Rafinesque, 1818), mean it was first described in a different genus.

Where the name comes from

Crotalus: from the Greek krotalon, a rattle or castanet - after the rattle at the tip of the tail[3]

viridis: ‘green’

Prairie Rattlesnake: ‘Rattlesnake’ – it shakes the rattle at the tip of its tail.
