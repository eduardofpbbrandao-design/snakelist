King Cobra

Ophiophagus hannah (Cantor, 1836)

The King Cobra is the world's longest venomous snake: a huge, alert elapid of the forests of South and Southeast Asia. Its scientific name Ophiophagus means snake-eater, and other snakes are indeed its main food. It is also the only snake known to build a nest for its eggs. In 2024 scientists split it into four species; SnakeList's page and map still cover the king cobra in the older, broad sense.[1,2,3]

Overview

Did you know?

  • It is the only snake known to build a real nest: the mother rakes leaves into a pile for her eggs.[5,1]
  • Its scientific name Ophiophagus means snake-eater.[8]

Who described it

First described by Cantor in 1836. The brackets in its name’s authority, (Cantor, 1836), mean it was first described under a different genus.

Identification

Appearance

A very long snake with smooth scales and a hood it spreads when alarmed. Adult colour depends on the region: pale olive in Malaysia, bronze-brown in eastern India, glossy black with pale crossbars in the Western Ghats, often with pale bands that converge towards the head.[8,9]

Young: Hatchlings look very different: jet black with bright yellow or white crossbands, which fade as the snake grows.[8,9]

Size

Typical adults: Adults typically measure 3-4 m, and individuals rarely exceed about 4.25 m.[1,4]

Largest record: The longest reliably reported specimen, caught in Thailand, measured about 5.6 m (18 ft 4 in).[4]

Variation

Regional: Colour and banding differ from region to region. These differences, together with DNA, are part of why the king cobra is now divided into four species (see Taxonomy).[6,3]

Range

Published distribution

Native (broad sense): From India and eastern Pakistan through Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Indochina and southern China to the Malay Peninsula, the Greater Sunda Islands and the Philippines.[1,3]

After the 2024 split: O. hannah in the strict sense is the Northern King Cobra of eastern Pakistan, northern and eastern India, the Andaman Islands, Indo-Burma and Indochina south to central Thailand. The Sunda King Cobra (O. bungarus) lives on the Malay Peninsula, the Greater Sunda Islands and parts of the southern Philippines; the Western Ghats King Cobra (O. kaalinga) in south-western India; and the Luzon King Cobra (O. salvatana) on Luzon in the Philippines.[3,10]

Ecology

Habitat

Mainly a forest snake: rainforest, bamboo thickets and mangrove swamps, often near streams and other water, from the lowlands up to about 2,000 m. It also turns up in plantations and farmland next to forest.[4,11]

Behaviour

Mostly seen hunting by day, though its activity varies with season and prey. When threatened it can raise about a third of its body off the ground and spread its hood.[4,1]

Diet

Almost entirely other snakes - including true cobras and, now and then, other king cobras - with monitor lizards taken occasionally.[1,2,8]

Reproduction

Lays eggs. The female gathers dead leaves into a mound using her body and tail, lays her eggs inside, and then stays nearby to guard the nest. Clutch sizes differ between reports, roughly 15 to over 20 eggs.[5,1]

Conservation

VUListed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List, with a decreasing population. That assessment was made before the 2024 split into four species, so it will need to be redone.[2,6]

It needs large areas of forest. A 2012 review reported local declines of more than 80 percent in parts of its range. After the 2024 split, the new species have not yet been assessed separately.[5,8]

Taxonomy

Family
Elapidae (cobras, mambas, coral snakes, sea snakes and relatives)
Genus
Ophiophagus – from the Greek words for snake and eater - because king cobras eat other snakes[7]
Species
Ophiophagus hannah (Cantor, 1836)

Long treated as a single species, the king cobra was shown by a 2021 DNA study to consist of four lineages. A 2024 revision in the European Journal of Taxonomy named them: O. hannah (Northern), O. bungarus (Sunda, an old name brought back), and two new species, O. kaalinga (Western Ghats) and O. salvatana (Luzon).[6,3]

Despite its name it is not a true cobra (genus Naja): it sits in its own genus and is more closely related to the mambas.[8]
