Green Tree Python

Morelia viridis (Schlegel, 1872)

The Green Tree Python is a small, stout, tree-living python of New Guinea, nearby islands and the tip of Cape York in northern Australia. It is famous for its colour change: babies are bright yellow or red and turn green as they grow. It is the most arboreal of the pythons.[1,2,3]

Identification

Appearance

Adults are bright green, often with a scattering of white or yellow scales along the back. It rests in a characteristic way, with its coils looped over a horizontal branch and its head in the middle.[3,1]

Size

Adults: Averages about 1.5 m in length; the largest recorded specimen was about 2.2 m. The Australian Reptile Park says it rarely exceeds 1.5-1.8 m.[1,4]

Weight: Captive males weigh about 1.1-1.4 kg and females up to 1.6 kg; wild snakes are usually much lighter.[3]

Variation

Colour change: Hatchlings are lemon yellow or brick red. They change to green as they grow - the CITES document gives a length of about 65 cm, and other studies a little less. The change can happen almost overnight or take several months, and is not tied to shedding.[2,3]

Yellow and red young: Yellow juveniles occur throughout the range, while red ones are found only in parts of New Guinea. A study found no difference between yellow and red juveniles in survival, sex ratio, body shape or diet.[3,5]

Similar species

  • Green Python Morelia azurea – the northern green python of northern New Guinea, split from this species in 2021; the two look alike and are separated mainly by range and genetics.[3]
  • Emerald Tree Boa Corallus caninus – the Emerald Tree Boa of South America looks and rests almost exactly like it - an example of convergent evolution - but is a boa that gives birth to live young.[3]

Ecology

Habitat

Tropical rainforest. Adults live in the canopy, while young snakes hunt nearer the ground.[3,1]

Behaviour

Adults are active mainly at night and spend the day coiled over branches. The switch from juvenile to adult colour coincides with a change in feeding: from hunting near the ground to hunting in the canopy.[1,3]

Diet

Mostly small mammals such as rodents, and sometimes reptiles such as geckos and skinks. Birds were once thought to be a major part of its diet, but field work has suggested they are not; some sources still list birds.[3,4]

Reproduction

Lays eggs, but breeding in the wild has never been documented.[3,1]

Captivity: In captivity females lay up to about 25 eggs (sources give ranges of 1-25 and 8-25), then coil around and protect them during incubation.[3,4]

Morelia viridis (Schlegel, 1872)

Long classified in its own genus as Chondropython viridis, it is now placed in Morelia. A study published in 2021 split the green pythons of northern New Guinea off as a separate species, Morelia azurea, leaving M. viridis for southern New Guinea, the Aru Islands and Australia.[3]
